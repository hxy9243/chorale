### Concrete voice-leading facts
Pitches below use scientific notation (C4 = middle C); each half note begins on beat 1 or 3.

- **Parallel compound fifths, S–B, m.1 beat 1 → beat 3:** soprano **G4→A4** and bass **C3→D3** both rise a whole step, preserving a perfect fifth plus an octave.
- **Direct compound octave, S–B, m.2 beat 1 → beat 3:** soprano **B4→C5** and bass **G2→C3** ascend together into a double octave. The soprano moves by semitone, not by leap.
- **Parallel perfect fourths, S–A:** **C5/G4** at m.2 beat 3 → **D5/A4** at m.3 beat 1 → **C5/G4** at m.3 beat 3. These are geometric parallels, but not parallel fifths.
- **No voice crossing or adjacent-voice overlap** occurs. S–A and A–T spacing remains within an octave throughout.

### Style-dependent concerns
- The **m.1 S–B parallel fifths** are the clearest issue under conventional strict SATB practice.
- The **m.2 direct octave** is often acceptable because the soprano approaches by step; it should not automatically be treated as a forbidden parallel.
- At **m.1 beat 3**, bass **D3** supports tenor **C4**, alto **F4**, and soprano **A4**. The **D3–C4 seventh** is dissonant. Tenor C4 is retained from the preceding sonority, then moves **up to D4 at m.2 beat 1**, rather than resolving downward. This is unconventional if treated as a chordal seventh in strict tonal writing.
- At **m.3 beat 3**, bass **G2** sounds beneath **E4–G4–C5**. The soprano’s C5 forms a compound fourth above the bass and remains C5 into m.4 while the bass moves to C3. This need not follow a cadential-six-four interpretation; under that interpretation, the usual upper-voice resolutions are absent.

### Limits
The notation fully specifies these four parts, but supplies no harmonic analysis or stylistic period. Dissonance treatment and direct intervals therefore require contextual judgment; parallel fourths alone are not an SATB fault.
