## The event across measures 4–5: a 4–3 suspension

The soprano's C5 is tied across the barline (`c4- | c B3`). In m4 that C is the **fifth of the F-major chord** — consonant. When the bass moves F→G at the start of m5, the held C5 now sounds a **fourth above the new bass G**, a dissonance. It resolves **down by step to B4 on beat 2 of m5**, the third of the G chord.

So this is a classic **4–3 suspension**: prepared (m4, consonant), sustained across the harmonic change (m5 beat 1), resolved down by step (m5 beat 2). Note that music21's evidence lists m5 beat 1 as a bare "G, other" sonority — that's the literal vertical slice, not a separate chord. It is simply the G chord with its suspension still sounding.

## Revision suggestion

Don't label m5 beat 1 as its own harmony. The student should annotate m5 as a single **G-major chord with a 4–3 suspension** (figure: V 4–3), showing the tied C as a non-chord tone resolving to B. Similarly, m6–7 can be heard as one motion (V–vi–V⁷ or simply a neighbor figure) rather than three unrelated verticalities — the ear tracks the bass line G–A–G and the soprano's stepwise resolutions, not each slice as a new function.
